# BC Legislature voting record and stated positions, 2016-2026 (for a neutral voter-advice tool)

Method note. Most vote tallies below come from the BC Legislative Assembly's own Votes and Proceedings (V&P) pages at lims.leg.bc.ca. Those pages list members by SURNAME only and do not state party. Party labels in the tables are my identification from the surname lists plus news reports about caucus membership at the time, and should be spot-checked before any individual MLA is named in the tool. Caucus membership changed a lot in 2023-2026 (BC Liberals rebranded as BC United in 2023; Conservatives expelled or lost members in 2025-2026; Greens had 2 MLAs). Where a stage passed "on division" or "nemine contradicente" without names, V&P gives no per-party breakdown, and I say so.

Labels used: NDP; BC Liberals (renamed BC United 2023, no longer a force after 2024); BC Conservatives; BC Greens (3 MLAs 2017-2024, 2 MLAs since 2024: Jeremy Valeriote, Rob Botterell); Independents/OneBC (Dallas Brodie, Tara Armstrong, and others, 2025-2026).

## 2. Recorded votes on landmark legislation, 2017-2026

### Takeaway
Key pattern from recorded divisions: the NDP voted for its own government bills throughout; BC Liberals/BC United opposed the spec tax (2018), the Surrey police bill (2023) and Bill 44 (2023) but backed Bill 47 and DRIPA; the Conservatives (2025-26) voted against Bills 7, 14, 15, the 2025 Supply Act and Budget Measures Implementation Act (No. 2), 2026; the Greens supported the NDP on confidence items until February 2026, and voted against Bills 14 and 15 (2025) and the 2026 budget bill at third reading.

### Cited Findings: 2017-2019

**Political donations ban (Bill 3, Election Amendment Act, 2017)**
- Third reading: no recorded division; "reported complete with amendment, and by leave, read a third time and passed" (2017-11-22). - [V&P 2017-11-22](https://lims.leg.bc.ca/pdms/ldp/41st2nd/votes/v171122.htm)
- Recorded divisions on an amendment to section 20 of Bill 3 and on s.20 as amended both passed 44-36. Yeas were NDP and Green MLAs (incl. Furstenau, Weaver, Olsen); the 36 nays were BC Liberal surnames (e.g. Rustad, de Jong, Wilkinson, Bond, Milobar). I did not check what section 20 does, so do not describe it. - [V&P 2017-11-22](https://lims.leg.bc.ca/pdms/ldp/41st2nd/votes/v171122.htm)
- The law ends corporate and union donations, caps individual donations at $1,200/yr, bans out-of-province donations. - [BC Gov news release](https://news.gov.bc.ca/releases/2017PREM0084-001592)
- Earlier, a BC Liberal bill to ban corporate and union donations was voted down 44-42 on 2017-06-26 when NDP and Greens opposed it at first reading (the Liberals said their own bill would end big donations; NDP/Greens said it was inadequate). - [CBC](https://www.cbc.ca/lite/story/1.4178353) (reported summary, details of stated reasons not verified)

### Inferences (labelled as inference)
- Housing: the record shows the NDP is the party that passed the supply-side mandates (Bills 44, 47, 46, 35, flipping tax). BC Liberals/BC United were not uniformly opposed to housing density (they voted for Bill 47, which includes density near transit) but opposed Bill 44's province-wide zoning override and Bill 46's financing changes. The Conservative platform and the caucus's 2023 votes indicate they favour less provincial override of local zoning and lower taxes/fees; check their 2026 housing platform file for the specifics.
- Fiscal: Conservatives voted against every budget-related bill in 2025-2026 (Supply Act 2025, Bill 7, BMIA No. 2 2026). Their stated reason is the deficit. A reasonable inference is that they would present a lower-spending budget, but their costed plan is a platform question, not a voting-record question.
- Greens: record shows conditional support, not loyalty. They voted yes on confidence items (2025 non-confidence, Supply Act, Bill 7) while the accord ran, and no on Bills 14/15 and on the 2026 budget bill at third reading after the accord ended. A voter weighing a Green MLA should expect issue-by-issue votes.
- NDP: record shows willingness to use time allocation (Nov 2023) and cabinet-order powers (Bill 14 expansion, Bill 7) to move quickly, and also willingness to reverse (DRIPA amendments shelved April 2026; decriminalization pilot ended January 2026).

## 3. Opposition motions, private members' bills, and responses to court rulings

### Takeaway
No Conservative bill to repeal DRIPA reached a vote. The recorded repeal-adjacent vote was OneBC's Property Rights Protection Act (first reading, 2025-10-29), defeated 40-48 with the Conservative caucus voting yes; the Conservatives say first-reading votes are procedural. The NDP planned to suspend parts of DRIPA in April 2026 and shelved it.

## 4. Did the BC Liberals support DRIPA in 2019, and how do Conservatives reconcile that now?

### Takeaway
Yes. The bill passed without any recorded opposition; the committee division was 82-0, and BC Liberal MLAs (including John Rustad, who was a Liberal MLA and former Aboriginal relations minister) did not oppose it. Rustad has said publicly that he voted for DRIPA and blames the circumstances; the Conservative Party now promises full repeal, and I found no formal party statement reconciling the two beyond Rustad's remarks.

### Cited Findings
- Committee division on Bill 41 on 2019-11-26: 82 yeas, nemine contradicente; no nays recorded; third reading passed without a recorded count. - [V&P 2019-11-26](https://lims.leg.bc.ca/pdms/ldp/41st4th/votes/v191126.htm)
- Media report: unanimous passage and MLAs on all sides applauding; Indigenous leaders had appealed to the BC Liberals earlier that day. - [Global News](https://globalnews.ca/news/6222331/british-columbia-passes-undrip/amp/); [Narwhal](https://thenarwhal.ca/bc-election-2026-dripa)
- Rustad (posted December 2025, as shown in search snippets; the X page itself would not load for me, so the wording below is a paraphrase from the search result and not a verified quote): "Yes I voted for DRIPA in 2019. I've never hidden from that", adding that he was not Liberal leader, did not write the platform or legislation, and voted with his caucus when the NDP held a majority. At a Smithers event he said he was given "no choice" by the BC Liberals. - [X post (not opened; snippet only)](https://x.com/JohnRustad4BC/status/2003192710057066515); [Tyee 2026-02-10](https://thetyee.ca/News/2026/02/10/First-Nations-Would-Not-Exist-Without-Canada-Rustad/) (not opened)
- Doerkson was first elected in 2020 as a BC Liberal and did not vote on DRIPA in 2019. - [Global News: party leaders](https://globalnews.ca/news/12068973/who-are-bc-party-leaders-2026-election/); [The Tyee 2026-10-09](https://thetyee.ca/Opinion/2026/10/09/Lorne-Repeal-DRIPA-Then-What/)
- The Conservatives' stated reason for repeal: DRIPA "has not provided the security it was meant to", "in court everywhere", certainty for property owners, and it duplicates Section 35; they promise partnerships without naming a replacement. - [Conservative BC DRIPA plan](https://conservativebc.ca/plan/first-nations-dripa/); [Global News 2025-12-08](https://globalnews.ca/news/11568633/bc-conservatives-david-eby-reconvene-legislature-repeal-dripa)
- A Fraser Institute piece and a Conservative-party piece characterize the 2019 support as a Liberal mistake based on NDP assurances (partisan sources). - [Fraser Institute](https://www.fraserinstitute.org/fr/commentary/bc-government-wants-quickly-make-major-changes-land-act); [Conservative BC page, title only](https://conservativebc.ca/the-ndp-said-what-about-dripa/) (not opened)

### Inferences
- Several current Conservative MLAs who were BC Liberal MLAs in 2019 (surname matches between the 2018 Liberal nay list and the 2025-26 Conservative lists: Rustad, Milobar, Wat, Paton) were in the Legislature in November 2019. I did not obtain the 82-name list, so I cannot say each one voted; they would not have been recorded as opposing. Treat as an inference to check at [V&P 2019-11-26](https://lims.leg.bc.ca/pdms/ldp/41st4th/votes/v191126.htm) before publishing names.
- The party's reconciliation, as stated, is: 2019 support was given under a different leader and platform and on assurances that DRIPA would not give an Indigenous veto; the December 2025 Court of Appeal ruling is cited as having changed DRIPA's legal effect (Halford: ruling gives UNDRIP "legal force across all B.C. statutes"). The NDP itself changed its position after the same ruling (to amend/suspend). A neutral tool can say: both major parties moved from the 2019 consensus after the 2025 court rulings, in different directions (amend vs repeal).
